Working Papers

Accusing and Believing in Equilibrium: Evidence from #MeToo [PDF]
with Bobak Pakzad-Hurson
Abstract We study how #MeToo changed the strength of sexual harassment accusations and adjudicators' willingness to believe them. #MeToo caused the win rate of sexual harassment complaints to rise by 10.1pp, potentially reflecting stronger complaints (selection) or more favorable adjudicators (direct treatment). We develop a framework to disentangle these effects using complaints filed before but resolved after #MeToo. We find #MeToo made adjudicators more favorable to complainants. Complaints induced by #MeToo were stronger. However, this masks significant heterogeneity: female induced reporters brought stronger complaints, while male induced reporters brought weaker complaints. That is, #MeToo encouraged unfounded accusations only by men.

Consolidation and Animal Welfare in US Meat Production [Slides]
with Camilla Schneier
Abstract US meat production is highly concentrated, raising concerns that extend beyond price to the humane treatment of animals. We build a novel panel of US slaughterhouses (2006-2024) from FOIA'd USDA inspection records, covering the universe of animal welfare violations and enforcement actions at meat facilities. We find that violations are concentrated among a small share of facilities, and that small, independently-run plants violate at much higher rates than large, professionalized ones. When a plant is acquired, its violation rate tends to shift toward that of its new parent company: acquisitions by high-violation firms raise violation rates at the acquired plant, while acquisitions by low-violation firms lower them. Linking slaughterhouses to Nielsen consumer purchase data, we show that meat sold at large grocery retailers is just as likely to come from a violation-prone facility as meat sold elsewhere, suggesting that consumers are largely unaware of the animal welfare record behind the products they buy.

Pre-Doctoral Work

Firm location, childcare, and the gender profit gap [PDF]
with Solène Delecourt, Anne Fitzpatrick, Layna Lowe
R&R at Strategic Management Journal
Abstract There is a significant gender profit gap among small firms in developing countries. How do a firm's initial conditions contribute to this disparity? If men and women start different businesses – operating in different locations or sectors – and those initial conditions are hard to change, it could limit overall profitability for women. In our representative sample of 3,077 businesses in Kenya, women's profits are 47% lower than men's. We identify two initial and sticky conditions contributing to this gap. First, women locate their businesses in less profitable locations, closer to home. Second, women operate in crowded sectors, whereas men are over five times more likely to be monopolists than women. These constraints are more binding for women with greater childcare responsibilities, emphasizing the importance of initial conditions in perpetuating the gender profit gap.

The impact of host race and gender on prices on Airbnb [PDF]
Journal of Housing Economics, 2019
Abstract This study investigates the impact of host race and gender on Airbnb property prices. I use an existing dataset of Airbnb listings and visually inspect 70,000 host profile pictures to code host demographics. I estimate that Asian hosts earn 4–5%, and Black male hosts 3%, less than White males for the same type of property. However, controlling for more observables weakens the effects, requiring a cautious interpretation of these point estimates. I use two proxies for the number of bookings a listing has to estimate whether a demand or supply shift is responsible for the price disparity. I find that despite the lower prices they charge for listings, minority hosts face lower demand. These findings are consistent with, but not conclusive of, the presence of discrimination.
